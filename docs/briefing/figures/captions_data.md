# Captions — data figures (fig3–fig7)

**Figure 3 | KPNA4 S60 (importin-α3) is a CK2 site, not a MAPK site.** (a) In the perturbation compendium, phosphorylation of KPNA4 S60 falls sharply with the CK2 inhibitor CX-4945 in all three cell lines where it was measured. In mitotic HeLa it is among the most strongly affected sites (z −7.0; rank 63 of 12,455 sites). In 25 experiments with verified ERK-pathway inhibition it does not fall (median z +0.19; 0 of 25 below −2). The z-score compares each site with all other sites in the same experiment. (b) Public chemical-genetic data (Menyhart et al. 2023; U2OS cells, n = 6 replicates). The selective CK2 inhibitor SGC-CK2-1 lowers S60 by −3.73 log2 at 4 h, and cells with an inhibitor-resistant CK2α recover +2.98 log2 of this (about 80%; FDR 4 × 10⁻⁴). The five other quantified members of the 14-site fast CK2 set behave the same way. As a reference, known (PhosphoSitePlus) CK2 substrates change only modestly. CX-4945 gives weaker rescue across the board, consistent with its off-targets. (c) The sequence around S60 is acidic and matches the CK2 consensus S-x-x-D/E.

**Figure 4 | Signalling programmes learned in cell lines are preserved in human tissue.** (a) Coherence is how strongly the phosphosites in a perturbation "footprint" co-vary across samples. It is shown for four footprints in normal adjacent tissue and in tumours from 6 CPTAC cohorts with paired samples (lines; protein- and 3-PC-adjusted). The reference is random sites matched on coverage and variability (grey; coherence ≈ 0.005). Labels give the median z against that reference. Only the DNA-damage programme gains coherence in tumours (meta z 6.3). (b) For 529 sign-defined relationships between programmes learned in the compendium, the fraction with the same sign in tissue as in cell lines. Chance is 0.5; the median over the 6 main cohorts is 0.91 in both normal tissue and tumours. *OV and UCEC have only 19 and 18 normal samples. (c) In the 7 cohorts with recorded excision-to-freezing times (n = 723 tumours), adjusting for measured ischaemia time leaves coherence unchanged (MEKi 0.170 → 0.169). Adjusting for a proxy "ischaemia score" roughly halves it (0.087). That score tracks the MAPK/stress axis itself, not the recorded time.

**Figure 5 | Changes in a perturbation's primary direction replicate across studies; reverse changes replicate weakly.** (a) Mirror test. A site changing in one study is scored as replicated if it changes the same way in another study of the same perturbation class. Replication is expressed as a log odds ratio against each study's base rate, so 0 means chance; error bars are 95% cluster-bootstrap CIs. Under kinase inhibitors (41 studies), decreases (the primary direction) replicate more strongly with effect size (0.32 → 0.77) than increases (0.23 → 0.35). Under stimulations (52 studies) the pattern flips: increases replicate more (0.56 → 1.62) than decreases (0.48 → 0.59). (b) Footprints built from one direction only are tested on held-out studies (AUROC; leave one study out; 0.5 = chance). Decrease sites transfer better for 12 of 16 inhibitor footprints, and increase sites for 9 of 12 stimulus footprints. The dashed line marks equal performance.

**Figure 6 | In tumours, genomic lesions shift the offset of the signalling cascade, not its gain, and receptor abundance is buffered.** (a) Schematic. An offset change moves output up at every input level; a gain change makes output respond more steeply to input. (b) Meta-analysed gain (standardised slope) of each cascade link across CPTAC cohorts (protein-adjusted; number of cohorts in brackets). Every link transmits (0.21–0.41). Receptor pY relates only weakly to ERK directly (0.10), against 0.17 expected from the two intermediate links. (c) Selected lesion tests from 162 pre-declared gain tests, none of which was significant (smallest q 0.99). Gain changes sit near 0, inside the ±0.15 band that spike-in simulations detected with 84–94% power. Offsets shift as expected, e.g. KRAS mutation +0.67 SD of ERK at a given receptor input (q 0.005; +0.60 after adding the ischaemia proxy). (d) At a fixed receptor pY level, tumours with more EGFR/ERBB2 protein have slightly lower ERK activation: meta-analysis −0.15 SD [−0.25, −0.05], p = 0.002, negative in 6 of 7 cohorts (n = 613 tumours), and −0.16 after adjusting for the ischaemia proxy.

**Figure 7 | Three caveated observations.** (a) Candidate mTORC1 sites on the lysosomal Rag–Ragulator machinery. Points show the median change in phosphorylation (z within experiment) under catalytic mTOR inhibitors, AKT inhibitors and S6K inhibitors; n = mTOR-inhibitor experiments. LAMTOR1 S98 falls to the 0.11th percentile of all sites (10 experiments, 2 studies from one lab), close to the direct mTORC1 site PRAS40 S183. LAMTOR1 S97 and RRAGC S15 rest on 1–2 experiments in this table. S6K inhibition was not verified in the S6K-inhibitor experiments, so their flat response cannot exclude S6K. (b) How fast a kinase substrate responds to acute inhibition reproduces between independent labs (split-by-lab Spearman ρ 0.27 for all substrates vs 0.15 for random sites and 0.17 for random sites matched for noise and dynamism). The effect holds for ERK and mTOR, more weakly for AKT (one lab pair), and fails for CDK (0.10, below random). Numbers under the bars are substrate counts. (c) For 5 ATP-competitive drugs, the cellular potency of downstream sites is 1.9–2.8 log units below the drug's ATP-free affinity (Davis 2011). This shift falls inside the band Cheng–Prusoff predicts for 1–5 mM intracellular ATP (grey), implying a median of 2.3 mM (1.5–3.6). With n = 5 drugs this is a consistency check, not a measurement. The allosteric MEK inhibitor selumetinib, which does not compete with ATP, shows no such shift.
